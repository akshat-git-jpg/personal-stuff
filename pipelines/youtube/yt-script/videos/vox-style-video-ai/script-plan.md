### SECTION: What Makes a Vox Style Video Look Like Vox

**NOTES**
- Goal: show what this style actually is, so the viewer can judge their own results later.
- Do not open the tool anywhere in this section. The tool arrives in the next one.
- Use real Vox clips, or your own finished clip. Never a made-up stand-in.
- The background never moves. Cut-out shapes move on top of it. That is what makes it feel like one long shot instead of a cut every two seconds.
- Example worth saying out loud: "a normal edit cuts to a new picture. This one just moves things around on the same picture."
- Rough paper texture, and a jerky low frame rate. Say clearly that the jerkiness is a choice, not a fault — people try to fix it.
- Five parts in every scene: text, main object, background, small extras, camera. Text pops on. The main object unfolds or pops. Small extras drift a little. The background holds still. The camera does one push-in or one slow pan, never both.
- Two moves the list misses: a map or arrow that draws itself, and a caption that lands exactly on the spoken word.
- Close on who it is for: finance, documentary, true crime, history, money and power. Vox's own topics are political and historical, backed by numbers.
- Be honest that it suits numbers and history, not everything. A product review, or anything needing two people arguing, is the wrong fit.
- No verdict card. This is a tutorial, nothing is being ranked.

### SECTION: How to Make a Vox Style Video with AI

#### Teaching the AI the Vox Style from Real Vox Videos

**NOTES**
- Goal: get the AI to read real Vox videos instead of guessing from memory. This is the step almost nobody does, so give it room.
- Set the problem up first: the AI already sort of knows what Vox looks like, and "sort of" is what gives everyone the same generic first attempt.
- The tool is VidIQ, a free YouTube research tool that pulls real data off YouTube. Thomas Creates uses it.
- Install: the VidIQ website, blue "install free extension" button at the bottom, add to browser. No manual connection needed.
- Inside Claude: the VidIQ drop-down in the prompt bar, then "unlock YouTube insights in Claude", then connect, then authorize. Four clicks — keep it quick, and cut the loading wait.
- The ask: break down exactly what gives the Vox style its look. Give the exact words you type in.
- What comes back is one research document: how shots are built, notes on getting good output, and ready-to-use prompt examples.
- It has two sets of prompts, one for pictures and one for movement. Say why they stay separate — the same word means different things to the two models. Example: "slow pan" means one thing to a picture model and something else to a video model.
- Test the breakdown against Joseph's seven-point checklist, built from studying 100 reference designs: colours, shapes, text, objects, big design, small detail, textures. If it does not name all seven, it is too vague — ask again.
- No pictures get made anywhere in this section.
- Close on reuse: you write this breakdown once, it feeds every video after it, and it is the one piece that survives when the tool changes.
- CTA — link in description, second body CTA, after the checklist lands. Point out the breakdown gets reused on every video.

#### Picking a Topic That Holds Up

**NOTES**
- Goal: pick a topic the style can carry, and gather the real numbers before writing a word.
- Nothing gets made here. It is preparation, in a plain notes file.
- The one rule that decides a topic: one voice has to carry the whole thing, with no cutting to a second person. If it needs two experts disagreeing, it is the wrong topic.
- Example that passes: "why this shipping route changed the world." Example that fails: "is this tool better than that one."
- The surprising angle gets written BEFORE the opening line, because the payoff is what the whole script builds toward. Write the hook first and you are guessing at what you are hooking toward.
- Skai's worked example: how an Amazon package reaches you in two days, with the angle that Amazon shipped it near you before you ordered it.
- Every figure goes in one list first, each checked at its source. Say this part is boring, then say what it buys — a video that survives the comments.
- Skai's script builder template does three things: tells the director the format, hands over the surprising angle, and gives it the checked facts with one firm instruction not to invent a single number beyond them.
- Put that instruction on screen as text. It is the most copyable line in the section. Without it you get a script full of believable figures nobody can check.
- Every number the viewer writes down here is real. This is where the video earns the right to say "fact-checked".

#### Writing the Script

**NOTES**
- Goal: get to a locked script, and show that the editing pass is what makes it good.
- Fill the template with the three things from the last section. Keep it quick — this step is fast because the last one was slow.
- What comes back is a real explainer shape: hook, buildup, payoff. Not a wall of text.
- Be fair: the first draft is usually already decent. Then say why decent is not finished.
- The beat that matters: you can click into the script and edit it like a document instead of asking the AI again. Skai: "I never take any AI's first pass as final."
- Explain the difference — asking again throws away the parts that were already right, editing keeps them.
- Never show the first script accepted as it came back. Edit two or three real lines on screen, in real time, so it reads as a document and not a chat.
- Then check every figure against your list, tighten it, and save. The director locks the script and holds it for everything that follows.
- Skai called this the hardest part of the whole video, and said you never touch it again afterwards.
- Say plainly that this is the one section where hurrying costs you later: every picture, every voice read and every movement pass is built on this script.

#### Locking the Look

**NOTES**
- Goal: show the generic first attempt in full, then the plain-English fix that rebuilds it. The recovery is the lesson, not the good version.
- A board is one still picture for one scene. The director makes a set of them from your script. Explain that once, the first time you use the word.
- Make the first set using the words from the style breakdown. Say what changed because of that step: the ask is specific now, not hopeful.
- Skai: do not just describe the style and hope it sticks. Make a few boards, pick the frames that nail the look, approve them.
- Name what is wrong with the weak result in plain terms: flat colour, plain icons, no texture, no cutout photos. Say this still happens even with a good breakdown — it is a starting point, not a failure.
- The fix is the most useful line in the section. Describe the look you want in ordinary words, name the actual colours, let it rebuild every board.
- Skai's real wording: "a modern collage look, real cutout photos in bold flat colour, grey subjects against coral and navy and cream." Before was flat blue boards with plain icons; after was bold collage frames with real cutout photos. This is the strongest visible result in the video.
- Approving the boards makes every later scene inherit the look without being asked again. Say what that saves.
- The text glitch: if captions keep coming back as nonsense, lock them as still graphics and animate only the objects. The director moves pictures well and struggles to redraw letters. Skai hit this hardest of the three failures he named.
- From here on, on-screen words stay still in every later shot.
- Close on the thing no tool replaces: left alone the output is flat, and the moment you bring a real visual idea it lifts. Luuk's swap — a map whose dots kept glitching, replaced with a person thinking over a chessboard. Zero glitches, and a better picture anyway. Luuk: "Trust your creativity. It is the one thing nobody can clone."

#### Picking the AI Voice

**NOTES**
- Goal: pick the narrator, and play real audio while doing it. A voice section with no voice in it is worthless.
- Ask the director for ten voices, five male and five female, each reading YOUR script — not a sample paragraph. A voice that sounds fine on a generic line can fall apart on your actual sentences.
- Skai: the first few sounded flat and a bit robotic, and the fourth was the one. What he picked was warm, calm, and a little curious.
- Play a flat read and a good read of the same line, back to back, weak one first and long enough to be uncomfortable.
- Name the difference the way a normal listener would. Example: "that one sounds like it is reading. This one sounds like it is telling you something." No audio jargon.
- The checklist is warmth, pace, and whether it sounds interested in its own subject. Land on that last one as the decider.
- Lock the voice, then say the thing that saves a re-record: you can redo a single line later without touching the rest. That is what makes locking safe rather than final.
- Say why the voice is cast before any movement exists — the narrator and the music set the mood before the pictures take over, and the temptation is to leave it until last.

#### Picking the Music

**NOTES**
- Goal: pick the track and lay down the timing base. Keep this shorter than the voice section — it works the same way.
- Ask for ten tracks with different moods. Skai's pick: warm and calm, with just enough pace to keep things alive without fighting the narrator.
- Music always plays under a real narration line, never on its own. Judged alone it is judged wrong.
- The one thing that decides a track is its pace against your read. Too slow and it drags you down, too busy and it fights you. Show both problems using the same line, loud enough that the problem is obvious before you name it.
- One sentence on copyright and takedowns, then move on. Skai's warning: strikes and takedowns are not worth the trouble, so keep your music original. Do not turn this into a lecture.
- Then lay the voice and music over all the boards in order. That is the timing base — the run of the whole video with the pictures still and nothing moving yet.
- What you get is a narrated slideshow. It fits together, it is on topic, and it is boring. Say that out loud, say it is supposed to feel flat, and say that flatness is the timing everything else gets built on.

#### Adding the Motion

**NOTES**
- Goal: add movement without wrecking the timing. Skai says this is where most beginners throw the whole project away.
- Open by defending the slideshow: it feels boring because the timing is doing its job and nothing is competing with it yet. Everything added from here has to earn its place against that.
- The rule: one simple movement per board, timed exactly to the line underneath it. The timing is what makes it feel deliberate, and the simplicity is what makes the timing readable.
- The common mistake is complicated animation on every board, hoping it works. Even when it works it looks over the top and the pictures distract from the words.
- A too-busy version has to be shown, full screen, and it has to be unpleasant. Name what it costs. Example: "the eye has nowhere to rest, and the words stop landing."
- Scale, with the real number: motion added to all 14 boards in one pass with one pasted prompt, in under 3 minutes, no batch limits. Make that about being able to try again, not about speed.
- The single-thing fix, which is what saves the project: type @, click the thing that is wrong, say what is wrong, ask for three to five versions, pick the one that got it right. Never remake a whole set to fix one shot, and never start over in a fresh chat.
- The harder failure, and the answer that sounds backwards: if a board keeps failing whatever you do, the board is the problem, not the prompt. Redesign the idea. The glitching map became a person thinking over a chessboard — zero glitches, and a stronger idea.
- Motion is timed to the voice, not to itself. It always puts a remade scene back into the right spot.

#### Putting the Final Cut Together

**NOTES**
- Goal: get the final cut out, held to the timing base.
- Ask the director to join it up, timed exactly as in the base, not re-timed. Say that condition out loud — this is what people get wrong.
- It always puts a remade scene back into the right spot, however many times it was redone.
- The review rule: watch it once, and look only for things that do not match between shots.
- Style is not on that list. It was locked four sections ago, and reopening it here means redoing everything after it.
- Two continuity failures worth naming, both from Luuk. Objects merging into one mid-shot — his case was two electricity towers. And a character who stops looking like the same person between scenes.
- Luuk's fix for the second is a plain instruction: make the person match the previous scene.
- Close on export and the few settings that matter. Keep it short — this is the least interesting beat in the video, and padding it would show.

#### How to Animate a Map Without It Glitching

**NOTES**
- Goal: give the best trick in the whole video, and the idea behind it, which works everywhere.
- Explain the failure first: told to animate a map, the model redraws it every frame, and the map stops being the same map.
- The fix is a before picture and an after picture, and letting the model move between them instead of drawing anything.
- Frame one is a clean map with no route. Frame two is the same map with a route arrow and a small paper tag added, and nothing else touched.
- Make frame two with add visual references, from references, attaching frame one. Then use the start and end frame option: frame one as start, frame two as end.
- Because the frames are identical apart from the route, there is nothing to redraw. Say the idea out loud: give the model less to redraw.
- Show the two still frames side by side FIRST, or the trick does not land. The finished shot comes after.
- For three frames instead of two: the start-and-end option only takes two images. Switch to the text with reference tab, open add visual references, go to from creations, load all three in order.
- Paste an animation prompt telling it to open on the empty version and build each part up in turn. Duration 15 seconds for that shot.
- Be honest about the fault Thomas hit: the map's colour and texture shifted slightly between frame one and frame two.
