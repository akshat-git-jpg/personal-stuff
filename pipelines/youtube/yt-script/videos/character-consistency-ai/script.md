### 7. The Character Consistency Test

**Voiceover**
> "Now the main event. Five scenes, same character, all five tools.
>
> Scene one: the baseline studio shot. Scene two: a rainy night city street, neon lighting. Scene three: a low-angle profile close-up. Scene four: a dynamic action shot, running down a hallway. Scene five: an animated clip, holding the identity through motion and dialogue.
>
> Here's what happened.
>
> InVideo AI breaks first, and it breaks hard. Because it's compiling from broad script prompts and stock modules rather than a locked character, the face and outfit change noticeably almost every scene.
>
> Atlabs holds up fine for the easy, conversational shots. But push it into the environment shift or the action shot, and the rendering engine starts drifting the age and facial proportions — it stops looking like the same person.
>
> Midjourney, using its current character reference system — Omni Reference, at maximum weight — keeps the face and the navy sweater intact through simple edits. But push it into that low-angle profile shot or the action pose, and you get subtle drift in the eye shape and jawline. It's close. Not perfect.
>
> Higgsfield's Soul ID is genuinely impressive here — it holds the facial bone structure rock-solid through every single angle change and the action shot. But Soul ID is built around the face specifically, and secondary details drift if you don't lock them hard in the prompt. In our test, that navy sweater turned into a black leather jacket the moment the environment changed.
>
> And OpenArt is the one that held everything. By saving the character into the Library and training a lightweight LoRA off ten to fifteen reference stills, then tagging the character in every prompt after that — the face, the hairstyle, the navy sweater, and the silver pendant stayed about ninety-five percent identical across all five scenes. Including the scene where the lighting completely changed to a dramatic neon street."

### 9. Style & Character Memory

**Voiceover**
> "Consistency isn't only the face. If the lighting, the color grade, and the overall look shift between scenes, the story still feels broken even if the character technically matches.
>
> Midjourney is the strongest here at pure style locking, using its style reference system. OpenArt matches that by stacking a custom Style LoRA directly onto your saved character profile, so the lighting and color grade stay identical scene to scene. Higgsfield's Cinema Studio lets you lock the lens and focal length too, so even the optical depth of the shot stays consistent.
>
> And then there's the practical question — how do you actually reuse this character next week, or on scene twelve of a twenty-scene story? Midjourney makes you manually copy and paste the reference image URL into every single prompt. Higgsfield lets you train a permanent Soul ID avatar once — takes about five minutes from twenty-plus reference photos — and it's done. Atlabs saves your character as a cast member inside the storyboard project. InVideo AI keeps preset project avatars and saved brand kits. And OpenArt gives you the most complete system of the five: a persistent Character Library, a simple tag you drop into any prompt, and a custom LoRA that stays saved in your account permanently."

**Notes**
[Demo placeholder — team to capture: compare scene backgrounds and color palettes across generated shots side by side. Then show OpenArt's @character tag entry next to Midjourney's manual URL-paste workflow.]

> **Verdict:** OpenArt and Midjourney both hold visual style well, for different reasons — Midjourney via reference locking, OpenArt via stacked Style LoRAs. On memory specifically, OpenArt's saved library beats everyone; Midjourney's manual URL workflow is the most repetitive of the five.

### 13. Honest Verdict

**Voiceover**
> "So here's my honest verdict, weakest to strongest.
>
> InVideo AI finishes last here on pure consistency — in this test, the character's face and outfit shifted noticeably between scenes. Worth flagging honestly: InVideo's own newer materials describe a 'persistent character sheet' system built directly for this problem, which sounds like a real answer to what we saw fail here. >
> Midjourney is still the best-looking single image of the five, by a clear margin. But it was never built to carry a character through a moving story — no native video, and even on stills, aggressive angles start to drift the face. Best tool here for concept art. Not the tool for a multi-scene story.
>
> Atlabs is the easy middle ground — simple storyboard casting, clean lip-sync, a genuine all-in-one pipeline. It just doesn't hold the character through a big environment or action change. Good for straightforward marketing clips. Not built for anything visually ambitious.
>
> Higgsfield does one thing better than anyone else here — Soul ID locks the actual face through wild camera angles better than any other platform in this test. The gap is everything around the face — the wardrobe drifts unless you fight it in every single prompt. The best tool if faces and motion are your priority, and you're willing to manage the wardrobe by hand.
>
> And OpenArt wins this comparison. Not because any single image is the most beautiful — Midjourney still takes that. It wins because it's the only platform that kept the face, the hair, the sweater, and the pendant recognizable across all five scenes, including a full lighting change, and then carried that same character straight into video without needing a second tool. Character Builder, a saved library, a real LoRA, and native video generation, all in one place. That's the actual job this video was testing, and OpenArt is the only one that did it end to end."
